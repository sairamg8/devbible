**Logging is the only observability signal that is written by hand, one statement at a time, by
people who will not be the ones reading it. Metrics are aggregates you design once. Traces are
generated by instrumentation. A log line is a message from a developer at 14:00 on a Tuesday to a
stranger at 03:00 on a Sunday, and the entire discipline of this topic is: write for that
stranger, and price the message honestly.**

## The three readers, and only one of them matters

Every log statement you write is read by one of three parties, and they want opposite things.

**The developer writing the feature.** Wants a running commentary — entering method, got the
value, about to save. Uses the log as a debugger because a debugger is inconvenient. This reader
is served by DEBUG, which is off in production, and their instinct is the source of most log
volume.

**The machine.** A log aggregator, an alert rule, a dashboard. Wants a stable schema, consistent
field names, and parseable values. It does not read prose. It is served by
[structured JSON](05-structured-json.md) and destroyed by a message whose shape changed because
someone reworded it.

**The stranger at 03:00.** Woken by a page, holding an alert that says error rate is up, with no
ability to reproduce anything and no context on the code. This reader has one question — *what
was the process doing, for which request, and why did it stop?* — and only the log can answer it,
because the failure already happened and everything else is gone.

🔴 **The third reader is the one you are writing for.** The first has a debugger and a local
machine. The second is served automatically once the schema is right. The third has nothing but
what you decided to write down months ago.

## What that reader actually needs from a line

Four things, and a line that lacks any of them is close to useless:

1. **Which request.** Not "which user" and not "which server" — *which unit of work*. Without a
   correlation identifier on every line, a busy service's log is an interleaving of hundreds of
   concurrent stories with no way to separate them. This is [MDC](06-mdc.md) and
   [correlation ids](07-correlation-ids.md), and it is the single highest-value thing in this
   topic.
2. **What was attempted.** Not "error occurred". The operation, and the identifiers that name the
   thing it was attempted on — order id, account id, message key.
3. **What happened instead.** The outcome, including the exception *object* rather than a
   stringified version of it ([09](09-exceptions-in-logs.md)).
4. **Enough to decide what to do next.** Retry? Page someone? Ignore? A log line that cannot
   discriminate between "transient, will self-heal" and "data is now wrong" has failed even if
   every field is populated.

## The test that catches most bad log lines

**Read the line with the code hidden.** If you cannot say what the process was doing without
opening the source file the line came from, the line is a comment, not a log.

```java
// Fails the test: means nothing without the source.
log.info("Processing");
log.debug("in loop, i={}", i);
log.error("Error: " + e.getMessage());

// Passes: the line is self-contained.
log.info("Reserving stock for order {} across {} warehouses", orderId, warehouseCount);
log.warn("Payment authorisation declined for order {}: issuer code {}", orderId, issuerCode);
log.error("Failed to publish OrderPlaced for order {} after {} attempts", orderId, attempts, ex);
```

The passing lines share a property: **they name an operation and the identity it operated on.**
That is what makes them searchable six months later by someone who has never seen the file.

## Logs, metrics and traces are not interchangeable

This is the boundary that keeps this topic from swallowing the next two, and it is also a real
engineering decision rather than taxonomy.

| | Answers | Cost model | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Log** | *What happened in this one execution?* | Per event — grows with traffic | This topic |
| **Metric** | *How often, how long, how many — across all executions?* | Per time series — grows with **cardinality**, not traffic | **08 · Metrics with Micrometer** *(not written yet)* |
| **Trace** | *Which services, in what order, and where did the time go?* | Per sampled request | **09 · Distributed tracing** *(not written yet)* |

🔴 **The failure mode is using a log to answer a metric's question.** "How many orders failed
today?" answered by counting log lines is a query over unbounded text that gets slower and more
expensive every day, and it silently becomes wrong the moment someone rewords the message. A
counter answers it in constant space forever. Conversely, "why did *this* order fail?" cannot be
answered by a counter at any cardinality you can afford — that is the log's job, and only the
log's.

**The practical rule: if you find yourself parsing a log message with a regex to build a
dashboard, you needed a metric.** If you find yourself adding a tag to a metric that has one
value per request, you needed a log.

## The log is a product with a bill

Nobody writes a log statement thinking about money, and every organisation of a certain size
eventually discovers that log ingestion is one of its larger infrastructure line items. The
arithmetic is unforgiving: a line is written once and then stored, shipped, indexed, replicated
and retained. The marginal cost of `log.debug` in a hot loop is not the nanoseconds it takes to
decide not to log — it is what happens the day someone turns DEBUG on to investigate something
and forgets to turn it off ([11](11-rolling-retention-and-cost.md),
[12](12-changing-levels-at-runtime.md)).

That gives the discipline a shape: **fewer lines, each carrying more.** One structured event with
eight fields beats eight prose lines, costs less to store, and is the only one of the two you can
query.

## The three commitments this topic argues for

Everything in the next twenty-six chunks reduces to three decisions, and they are worth stating
up front because each one is unpopular the first time it is proposed.

**1 · One event, one object.** A unit of work emits a structured record with named fields, not a
scattering of prose across five statements. [05](05-structured-json.md) argues this and
[05b](05b-wiring-json-in-spring-boot.md) wires it.

**2 · Context comes from the context, not the signature.** The request id, tenant and user do not
get threaded through every method parameter — they live in [MDC](06-mdc.md) and appear on every
line automatically. The price is a `ThreadLocal` on a pooled thread, which is
[a genuine leak](06b-mdc-and-thread-pools.md) and the most common bug in this topic.

**3 · What is not logged is a design decision, not an omission.** Credentials, tokens, card data
and personal data are excluded deliberately, by mechanism rather than by discipline
([08](08-what-never-to-log.md), [08b](08b-masking-and-the-audit-trail.md)). A log file is a
copy of your data with none of your database's access controls on it.

## Where this topic sits in an incident

The phase's gate scenario — *p99 latency doubled after the deploy* — has an ordered plan, and
logging is not step one. Metrics say **that** something changed and **when**. Traces say **where**
the time went. The log says **what the service was doing for the one request you finally picked
out**, and it is the only signal that can tell you the request was rejected because a field
failed validation.

That ordering is also why an over-logged service is worse than a quiet one: at 03:00 you are
searching, and the cost of a search is set by the volume of everything you wrote that did not
matter.

## Gotchas

**★ Logging for yourself instead of for the on-call.**
"Entering `processOrder`" is a note to the author during development. It survives into production,
where it costs storage forever and answers no question anyone will ask. Ask what a stranger would
need; delete the rest.

**★ A message that only makes sense next to the source file.**
`log.debug("here")`, `log.info("ok")`, `log.warn("retrying")` — none of these can be read without
opening the code. In an incident nobody opens the code first; they search the log.

**★ Using log volume as a proxy for observability.**
A service that logs ten lines per request is not better observed than one that logs one good
structured event. It is harder to search, more expensive to keep, and more likely to have its
useful line buried.

**★ Counting log lines to answer a question a counter should answer.**
Dashboards built on `grep`-style queries over free text break the day the message is reworded and
get slower as retention grows. That is a metric's job — **08 · Metrics with Micrometer**
*(not written yet)*.

**★ Adding a per-request identifier as a metric tag.**
The mirror-image error. Request id, user id and order id have unbounded cardinality; as metric
tags they blow up the time-series database. They belong in the log, where per-event cost is the
model.

**★ Treating the log as free because the write is fast.**
The write is cheap; the ship, index, replicate and retain are not. Cost is incurred downstream and
shows up on a bill nobody attributes back to the statement that caused it.

**★ Assuming the log survives the incident.**
Console output from a pod that gets rescheduled is gone unless something collected it. If the
answer to "where do these lines go" is "the container's stdout", the answer to "can I read them
after the crash" may be no. [10](10-appenders-and-async.md) owns that argument.

**★ Logging the same failure at three layers.**
Repository logs it, service logs it, controller advice logs it. One incident, three stack traces,
three times the storage and a reader who cannot tell whether three things failed or one did.
[09](09-exceptions-in-logs.md) has the rule that prevents it.

## Interview questions

**★ Who is the audience for a production log line, and why does it matter?**
Someone who did not write the code, cannot reproduce the failure, and is reading under time
pressure after an alert. It matters because the natural audience — the developer writing the
feature, using the log as a debugger — wants a completely different thing: a running commentary
at DEBUG that is off in production and would be worthless if it were on. Almost every logging
pathology, from prose messages to per-method entry logs, comes from writing for the wrong reader.

**★ When should something be a log line and when should it be a metric?**
If the question is about one specific execution — which order, which request, why did *this* one
fail — it is a log, because that question needs identifiers that have unbounded cardinality and
per-event cost is the right model. If the question is an aggregate over all executions — how
many, how often, how long — it is a metric, because a time series answers it in constant space
regardless of traffic. The tell that you got it wrong in one direction is a dashboard built by
regex over log text; the tell in the other direction is a metric tag holding a request id.

**★ Your service logs ten lines per request and the on-call still cannot diagnose failures. What
is wrong?**
Almost certainly that the ten lines are narration without identity: no correlation id tying them
to one request, prose messages that only make sense beside the source, and the actual outcome
spread across statements so that no single line says what was attempted and what happened. The
fix is not more logging — it is fewer, denser events with a correlation id and named fields, so
one query returns the whole story of one request.

**★ Why is "we log everything, so we are covered" a bad position?**
Because search cost, storage cost and signal-to-noise all move against you, and none of them
show up at the moment you write the statement. At 03:00 the constraint is not whether the
information exists somewhere; it is whether you can find it in the first two minutes. Volume
directly attacks that. It also raises the odds that something you should never have written down
— a token, a card number, an email address — ends up in a store with weaker access control than
the database it came from.

**★ What four things does a good production log line contain?**
Which unit of work it belongs to (a correlation identifier, from MDC rather than from a
parameter), what was attempted including the identifiers of the thing acted on, what happened
instead including the exception object rather than its string form, and enough for the reader to
choose an action — retry, escalate, or ignore. A line missing the last one is common and is why
so many WARNs get ignored.

**★ Why is the log the last of the three signals in an incident rather than the first?**
Because metrics tell you *that* something changed and when, and traces tell you *where* the time
went — both of which narrow the search before you start reading text. The log answers the final
question, "what was this particular execution doing", which is only tractable once you know which
execution to look at. Reading logs first means searching an unbounded corpus with no filter.
