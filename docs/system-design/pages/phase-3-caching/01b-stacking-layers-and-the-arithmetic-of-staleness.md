**When caches are stacked, two numbers compose and a third does not: the worst-case staleness is
the *sum* of the layers' lifetimes, the fraction of requests that reach the origin is bounded by
the *product* of the layers' miss ratios only if the layers were independent — and they never
are, because each layer in front removes exactly the easy traffic the layer behind would have
hit.** [01](01-where-a-cache-can-live.md) placed the six layers; this page is what happens when a
single request passes through several of them, and how to design the stack so the sum is
something you chose.

## Staleness adds up

Take a read that passes through an in-process cache, then a distributed cache, then the database.
Suppose each cache fills by reading the layer behind it and then holds the entry for a fixed
time-to-live, and neither knows how old the entry it received already was. Let the in-process
lifetime be `T1` and the distributed one `T2`.

The entry in the distributed cache can be up to `T2` old when read. The in-process cache fills
from it at that moment, treats it as brand new, and serves it for `T1` more. So the worst case a
reader can see is an entry that was `T2` old when it was copied and then lived `T1` in the
second layer: **`T1 + T2` behind the truth**, even though neither layer's own configuration says
anything larger than its own number. Add a CDN with lifetime `T3` in front and a browser with
`T4`, each filling from the one behind without accounting for age, and the bound is
`T1 + T2 + T3 + T4`. Each team set a reasonable number for its own layer; nobody owns the sum.

A design therefore states the staleness budget **per data type, end to end**, then allocates it:
the price may be wrong for at most some stated window, so the sum of every layer's lifetime on
the price's path must fit inside it, or the layer must be one you purge. This is the same
argument as a latency budget, applied to freshness.

### HTTP does the accounting; your own layers do not

HTTP caches were designed for chains, and the protocol carries the age along so the sum does not
silently grow:

> *"A response's 'age' is the time that has passed since it was generated by, or successfully validated with, the origin server."* — RFC 9111 §4.2.3

Age counts from the origin, not from when the *last* cache stored the response. A CDN that
received a response already some seconds old reports that in the `Age` header, and a browser
computing freshness uses it, so a long chain does not hand the user a response that is fresh by
its own clock and stale by the origin's. This is the property that makes HTTP lifetimes
composable: the lifetime is measured against the origin's clock, not each layer's.

The in-process and Redis layers you build do **not** do this unless you make them. A Redis entry
written with a TTL records when *Redis* stored it, and the in-process layer that reads it has no
way to know whether the value was produced just now or lived nearly its whole lifetime. Two
repairs:

- **Store the production time with the value** (a timestamp field inside the cached object) and
  have the layer in front set its own expiry as `min(T1, produced_at + budget - now)`. The
  front layer then inherits the age the way HTTP does.
- **Use Redis's remaining TTL** when filling the front layer: ask for the key's remaining time
  and cap the local lifetime at it. The sum becomes a maximum, at the cost of a second call.

Neither is free, and neither is usually done; the usual answer is to make the front lifetime
short relative to the back and accept the sum. What matters in an interview is that you know the
sum exists.

## What the origin sees

Let the layers have miss ratios `m1`, `m2`, … `mn` — the fraction of requests *reaching* a layer
that it fails to answer. If the layers were independent, the fraction of all requests that reach
the origin would be the product `m1 × m2 × … × mn`, and every extra layer would multiply the
protection. That is the argument for a stack, and it is the argument that is only half right.

**The layers are not independent.** The first layer to see a request answers the repeats — the
popular, recently-seen keys it holds. What it passes back is the traffic *it could not answer*:
first reads, rare keys, the long tail, and keys that expired. The second layer then sees a
population enriched in exactly the requests that are hard to cache. Its miss ratio, measured
against what it receives, is therefore **worse than it would have been on the raw traffic**, and
the product `m1 × m2` overstates the protection of the pair.

Two consequences follow, and they are the reason the arithmetic is worth saying out loud:

1. **A layer's hit ratio measured alone says little about the stack.** A distributed cache
   behind a good in-process cache can show a low hit ratio and still be doing its job — it is
   serving the tail the front layer by design does not hold. Judging it by the hit ratio it would
   have had alone is the mistake; the measuring page of this phase (**13 · Measuring a cache**
   *(not written yet)*) says what to look at instead.
2. **Removing a front layer does not just move its hits to the next layer.** It hands the next
   layer the popular keys it was shielded from. The load on the backing store changes by more
   than the front layer's hit ratio suggests, because the hits being returned are concentrated
   on a few keys — the shape that causes hot-key trouble (**06 · Hot keys and skew**
   *(not written yet)*).

The rule of thumb is directional and needs no number: **each layer reduces the load behind it,
by less than its own hit ratio would suggest in isolation, and the reduction is greatest for the
repeated keys and least for the tail.** The origin must be sized for the tail plus the cold
case, not for the average.

## The cold stack

A stack's steady-state numbers describe a warm system. The design is tested by the cold one,
and there are three distinct cold events with different consequences.

| Event | Layers emptied | What the next layer sees |
|---|---|---|
| a rolling deploy | the in-process caches, one instance at a time | a bounded stream of misses from the restarting instance; the distributed cache absorbs it if warm |
| a CDN purge of a popular object | that object, at every edge at once | every edge's next request for it, converging on the origin unless coalesced |
| the distributed cache restarted or flushed | all shared entries | the whole read workload from every instance, minus what the in-process layer still holds |
| all of the above together (a bad release) | everything | the database, with no net |

The last row is the only one with no layer behind it, and it is the case that decides whether the
system survives its own deploy. The design responses are in order of cheapness: roll
instead of restarting all at once; keep the in-process layer for the keys whose loss would be
worst; warm the distributed cache for the known-hot keys before cutting traffic over; and make
the origin's behaviour under overload explicit (shed load, serve stale, queue) rather than
whatever happens when connections run out. Coalescing concurrent misses is the stampede page
(**04 · Stampede and thundering herd** *(not written yet)*).

## When a layer is down, not just cold

A cold layer answers, slowly. A layer that is unreachable forces a decision the code must make
on every request, and the decision should be made in the design, not in the exception handler.

- **Fail open to the origin** — the cache error is treated as a miss and the request goes to the
  database. The system stays correct and the database takes the load; right when the origin can
  survive the load, and wrong when the cache exists precisely because it cannot.
- **Fail closed** — return an error. Correct and safe for the origin, visible to the user;
  occasionally right for data that must not be read stale and has no fallback.
- **Serve stale** — if a stale copy is held in a layer still reachable (the in-process one, a
  CDN with `stale-if-error`), use it and mark it. The cheapest degradation, available only if a
  stale copy was retained rather than deleted on expiry.

Whichever is chosen, put a **timeout on the cache call** that is much shorter than the request's
budget. A cache that hangs is worse than one that is down: the request waits on it, then also
goes to the origin, and the cache has turned a miss into added latency on every request. The
mechanism is the retry-and-timeout discipline of
[timeouts, retries and budgets](../phase-2-request-path/08-timeouts-retries-and-budgets.md);
the cache is one more dependency with the same rules.

## Consistency: what each layer can promise

Different layers can promise different things about what a reader sees after a write, and a
design names which promise each data type needs.

| Promise | What it means | Layers that can keep it |
|---|---|---|
| **Read-your-own-write** | after I save, my next read shows it | any layer you invalidate or bypass for that user on that write; the browser cannot unless the URL changes or the client updates its own copy |
| **Bounded staleness** | no reader sees data older than a stated window | any chain whose summed lifetimes fit the window, or whose layers are purged |
| **Eventual** | readers converge once writes stop | every layer with some expiry or invalidation |
| **Strong** | every read sees the latest write | the database itself; a cache only if every read is verified against the source |

The storefront needs different promises for different data: a user's own cart is
read-your-own-write; a product description is bounded staleness measured in minutes; the price
shown on the listing is bounded staleness measured tighter, *and* the price charged at checkout
is read from the source. That last split — cache the read, verify at the write — is the page
**08 · What not to cache** *(not written yet)*; the point here is that "cached" is not one
consistency level, and each layer can be assigned the weakest promise the data tolerates.

## The storefront's map

One example of the stack, per data type. This is a worked decision rather than the final plan
(the phase's gate is that plan); the point is that **different data types sit in different
layers and the lifetimes are chosen against a staleness budget**.

| Data | Layers it passes through | Why |
|---|---|---|
| product image, JS bundle | browser (long, immutable), CDN | content-hashed names; never changes under its URL; the origin sees almost none of it |
| product description | CDN (short lifetime, purge on edit), distributed cache | changes rarely, tolerates a window; the edit event is the purge trigger |
| product price (listing) | CDN (very short or purged on change), distributed cache | the hard case; the budget is tight, so purge carries the load and the lifetime is only the net |
| product price (checkout) | none — read from the database | money; see **08 · What not to cache** *(not written yet)* |
| category tree, currency table | in-process, with a short lifetime | tiny, hot, read on every request; a few seconds of disagreement between instances is harmless |
| search results | distributed cache keyed by normalised query, short lifetime | too many keys and too personal for the edge; popular queries repeat |
| cart | distributed cache or the database, per-user key | private, read-your-own-write; never shared |
| the flash-sale SKU | every layer, plus a plan | one key read by everyone at once: the edge holds it, a local copy absorbs the rest, and the pre-sale warm-up decides whether the first second is survivable |

Note what is absent: no data type uses every layer. The layers are a menu, and the plan is a
choice from it per row.

## Gotchas

**★ Symptom: a price was corrected and a support ticket shows a customer still saw the old price
long after both caches' TTLs should have expired.** Cause: stacked lifetimes add; the entry spent
its full lifetime in one layer and was then copied into the next as if new, so the worst case is
the sum of the lifetimes on the path, not the largest. Fix: write the end-to-end staleness
budget for the data type, make the layer in front shorter than the layer behind, propagate
production time through your own layers, and purge the layers you can reach.

**★ Symptom: the second-level cache shows a poor hit ratio and someone proposes removing it.**
Cause: judged in isolation; the layer behind a good front cache sees only the traffic the front
layer could not answer, which is the hard part. Fix: judge it by what reaches the origin with and
without it, and by the tail latency it protects, not by its own hit ratio.

**★ Symptom: removing the in-process cache "to simplify" made Redis latency and CPU rise far more
than the in-process hit ratio implied.** Cause: the in-process layer was absorbing the hot keys,
which are a large share of requests and all land on a small number of Redis keys (and on one
shard of a cluster). Fix: measure per-key request concentration before removing a front layer;
keep it for the hot few.

**★ Symptom: the system is healthy for weeks and the first bad deploy takes the database down.**
Cause: the origin was sized for the warm miss rate; a restart emptied every layer together.
Fix: treat the cold start as a design case with an owner — staggered restarts, warm-up for the
hot keys, origin overload behaviour defined, and a game-day that flushes the shared cache on
purpose.

**Symptom: a cache outage made every request slower than having no cache at all.** Cause: no
timeout (or a long one) on the cache call, so every request waited on the dead layer and then
went to the origin. Fix: a cache timeout far shorter than the request budget, and a circuit that
treats a failing cache as a miss for a window without waiting each time.

**Symptom: a single read returns different values on consecutive refreshes.** Cause: requests
alternate between instances whose in-process caches were filled at different times, or between
edges with different ages. Fix: if it cannot be allowed, serve that data from one shared layer
or session-pin; otherwise bound the difference with a short lifetime and accept it.

**Symptom: "we cache everywhere" and no one can say what the staleness of a given page is.**
Cause: layers added independently; the sum was never computed. Fix: one line per data type
stating the layers, the lifetime at each, the end-to-end bound and the purge path; review it
whenever a layer is added.

**Symptom: a staleness bug appears only for the user who just made the change.** Cause:
read-your-own-write is not provided by any of the layers, and the write path invalidated one
copy while the user's next read hit another. Fix: after a write, either bypass the caches for that
user's next read (a short-lived flag) or have the write return the new representation and update
the client's copy.

## Interview questions

**★ If the in-process cache lives for 30 seconds and the Redis entry for 5 minutes, how stale can
the data be?**
Up to the sum, not the larger: an entry that has lived nearly its whole five minutes in Redis can
be copied into the in-process cache and then served for another thirty seconds, so the worst case
is five and a half minutes. Neither layer's configuration shows that number. HTTP avoids this
because the `Age` header carries time since the origin generated the response (RFC 9111 §4.2.3),
so freshness is measured against the origin's clock. A hand-built stack does not, unless the
production timestamp is stored with the value and the front layer caps its own lifetime by what
remains.

**★ Why can a second cache layer have a low hit ratio and still be worth keeping?**
Because it sees only what the first layer missed. The first layer answers the repeats; what it
passes on is the long tail, the first reads and the expired entries, which are inherently harder
to hit. A low ratio on that population is expected. The question that decides whether to keep it
is what reaches the origin with it and without it, and what its misses cost.

**★ What do you size the origin for in a cached design?**
The tail plus the cold case, not the average. The warm hit ratio describes a good day; the origin
must survive the concurrent misses after a deploy, a flush or a purge, which is the whole read
workload minus whatever layer survived. If it cannot, the design needs warm-up, staggered
restarts, coalescing of concurrent misses, or an explicit overload behaviour such as serving stale
or shedding load.

**What do you do in the code when the cache is down?**
The decision belongs in the design: fail open to the database if it can take the load, fail
closed if the data must not be read from anywhere else, or serve a retained stale copy if one
exists. In every case the cache call has a timeout much shorter than the request budget and the
failing cache is treated as a miss for a window, because a slow dead cache is worse than a
fast absent one.

**How would you give a user read-your-own-write when several caches sit in front of the
database?**
Not by invalidating every layer, which cannot be done for the browser. Either the write's response
carries the new representation and the client updates its own copy, or the user's next read
bypasses the caches (a short-lived marker on the session or a version in the request), or the
reads for that user go to the source. The point is that the guarantee is per user and per request,
and it is cheaper to bypass than to purge.

**Which layers do you reach for on the flash-sale SKU?**
Every one that can hold a single public key read by everyone at once: the CDN edge for the
product page with a short lifetime and coalescing so a miss is one origin request, an in-process
copy so the hot key does not land on one Redis shard, and a warm-up before the sale so the first
second is not the cold case. The stock count and the price charged are not cached reads; they are
verified at the write.
